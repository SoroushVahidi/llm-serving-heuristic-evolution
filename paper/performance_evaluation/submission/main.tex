\title{When Does LLM-Serving Scheduler\\
Adaptation Matter?\\
Action Opportunity and Causal Headroom\\
in Production-Derived Replay}

\author{Soroush Vahidi}

\address{New Jersey Institute of Technology, Newark, NJ, USA}

\begin{abstract}
Adaptive scheduling for large language model (LLM) serving pays off only if the live system reaches states where a different executable action exists and taking it improves performance. We measure it before any adaptive scheduler is designed. In a deterministic simulator replaying production-derived traces (Azure code, Azure conversation, BurstGPT), we count how often a six-policy portfolio proposes an action different from the single best scheduler's and, on fresh non-overlapping windows, force one alternative action for one step before returning to the default. Under abundant resources, replay was action-null in about one million decision states. Capping key-value (KV) cache capacity or concurrent sequences created disagreement; scaling arrival rates up to eight times did not. In a preregistered analysis of 720 fresh disagreement states, 590 (81.9\%) had positive one-step latency headroom; the state-weighted mean oracle headroom, the best available one-step latency reduction, was 2.00 ms (95\% window-clustered confidence interval 0.19--3.55 ms). Post hoc analyses show that the sign is stable across weightings and leave-one-out analyses, but the magnitude is not: the median is 0.20 ms, and one of 36 windows holds 88\% of the total headroom. A bounded vLLM probe shows that resource pressure changes scheduler behavior but does not validate the simulated latency effect. Adaptation opportunity is real but sparse, pressure-dependent, and generally modest; detecting it does not show that a deployed adaptive scheduler is worthwhile.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Adaptive scheduling in large language model (LLM) serving is usually motivated
by heterogeneous prompts, decode demand, key-value (KV) cache residency,
queueing, and deadlines. It can help only if the live system reaches states
where a different executable action exists, such as another admission, batch,
or request order, and only if taking that action improves the target metric by
more than the cost of choosing it. How often this happens is rarely measured
before a scheduler is designed.

Common evaluation practice does not answer this. Ranking policies on
whole-trace metrics, or reporting the gap to a virtual best solver (VBS) as in
algorithm portfolios~\cite{rice1976algorithmselection,gomes2001portfolios},
shows that policies differ but aggregates thousands of decisions; it cannot say
whether an alternative was available at a given state. Policies with different
scores can issue the same batch, and an alternative that exists may not reduce
latency. Recent systems address \emph{how} to
adapt~\cite{llumnix2024,sola2025,distserve2024,lmetric2026}. We ask the prior
question: \emph{when is there a different useful action to take, and how much
is it worth?}

We answer it with a staged decomposition,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \text{resource pressure}&\longrightarrow P(D)\longrightarrow
  P(B_{\mathrm{LAT}}\mid D)\\
  &\longrightarrow\text{headroom magnitude}\\
  &\longrightarrow\text{predictability and deployment}.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:chain}
\end{equation}
Here $D$ means that at least one canonical executable alternative differs from
the default single best scheduler (SBS), and $B_{\mathrm{LAT}}$ that at least one
such alternative lowers one-step mean latency relative to that default. These
are distinct quantities: pressure can make policies disagree often without the
disagreement being useful, and useful disagreement can be rare or small. The
last arrow, whether a selector could exploit the opportunity, is not measured
here.

This is a performance-evaluation question: at which operating points does
scheduler choice have causal performance consequences? Answering it takes
workload characterization, controlled resource interventions, replay-based
simulation, and inference that respects the dependence among states from one
trace window. Recent work in \emph{Performance Evaluation} treats job
assignment for machine-learning inference~\cite{choudhury2025job}, dispatching
policies on Google and Alibaba workloads~\cite{yildiz2026dispatching}, latency
prediction for GPU-accelerated model serving~\cite{liang2026lilou}, and
adaptive machine-learning training on serverless
clouds~\cite{ali2025enabling}; our measurement-first view characterizes the
region in which such mechanisms have something to choose between.

We replay production-derived traces (Azure 2023 code and
conversation~\cite{azurepublicdataset2023}, BurstGPT~\cite{burstgpt2025}) in a
deterministic simulator. Faithful replay of 60 windows gives the native
baseline; one-axis interventions on active-sequence capacity, KV capacity, and
arrival rate (1,080 conditions) locate where disagreement appears. On fresh
non-overlapping windows we clone the simulator at each disagreement state,
force one alternative action once, and return to the default. The primary
endpoint, oracle latency headroom (the largest one-step mean-latency reduction
among the alternatives, zero if none helps), was fixed in a preregistered
protocol; the robustness analyses of the frozen results are post hoc. A bounded
vLLM~\cite{vllm2023} probe checks the mechanism in a real engine.

Native replay is action-null under abundant resources. Capacity caps on active
sequences or KV cache create disagreement; arrival-rate scaling up to eight
times does not. Of 720 fresh disagreement states, 590 (81.9\%; 589 excluding one
floating-point-residue state) had positive headroom, with a state-weighted mean
of 2.00 ms (95\% window-clustered confidence interval 0.19--3.55 ms). The sign
is robust: the mean stays positive under equal-window, equal-regime, and
equal-workload weighting and when any single window or regime is removed. The
magnitude is not uniform: the median is 0.20 ms, only 22.5\% of states offer
more than 2 ms, and one of 36 windows carries 88\% of the total headroom. The
vLLM probe shows that pressure alters queueing and scheduling behavior but does
not validate the simulator's latency magnitudes. Adaptation opportunity is thus
real but sparse, pressure-dependent, and generally modest, and detecting it
does not show that a deployed scheduler is worthwhile. The evidence covers three
trace families, a six-policy portfolio, a simulator, and one bounded probe on a
single model and GPU; the oracle is an opportunity bound, not a scheduler.

The study addresses five research questions, ordered along the decomposition.
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1 -- Native opportunity.] How often does the SBS face a different
executable action from the six-policy portfolio, in faithful replay of
production-derived traces with abundant resources?
\item[RQ2 -- Pressure-induced opportunity.] Which resource-pressure dimensions
create action disagreement, and does the transition reproduce on untouched
windows?
\item[RQ3 -- Causal value.] At disagreement states, how often does forcing one
alternative action for one step reduce latency, and does the answer depend on
the objective?
\item[RQ4 -- Magnitude and concentration.] How large is the headroom, is it
typical or concentrated, and how sensitive is it to weighting and to individual
windows or regimes?
\item[RQ5 -- System correspondence.] Does a bounded vLLM probe show the
corresponding pressure and scheduling mechanism?
\end{description}

\subsection{Contributions}
This paper makes five contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A staged action-opportunity evaluation that separates executable
  disagreement, beneficial disagreement, and headroom magnitude from
  policy-score diversity, with prevalence measured over all valid decision
  states and uncertainty quantified by trace window.
  \item A characterization of where disagreement appears: native replay is
  action-null, and only capacity limits on active sequences or KV cache, not
  arrival-rate scaling, expose alternatives; the transition reproduces on
  untouched windows.
  \item A preregistered one-step causal measurement on 720 fresh states, with
  latency as the primary endpoint because deadline goodput saturates at 1.0 and
  hides latency differences.
  \item A robustness and concentration analysis showing that the sign of the
  headroom is robust but its magnitude is concentrated, so a mean reported
  without its distribution and cluster composition overstates the typical
  opportunity.
  \item Bounded system evidence that resource pressure changes queueing and
  scheduling behavior in vLLM, without validating simulated latency magnitudes
  or a deployed selector.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} positions the work, and Section~\ref{sec:methods}
defines the estimands and evidence design. Sections~\ref{sec:phasea}--\ref{sec:fresh}
report the native, pressure, and fresh causal results, Section~\ref{sec:robust}
tests their robustness and concentration, Sections~\ref{sec:anwg}
and~\ref{sec:real} cover objective sensitivity and the vLLM probe, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the evidence, its practical
implications, and its limits before Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work and Positioning}
\label{sec:related}

LLM-serving research spans model execution, prefill/decode scheduling,
resource management, routing, caching, and cluster control. Orca and vLLM
established iteration-level serving and paged KV management
\cite{orca2022,vllm2023}. Sarathi-Serve, DistServe, Splitwise, and FastServe
study chunking, phase separation, and preemptive scheduling
\cite{sarathi2024,distserve2024,splitwise2024,fastserve2026}. Llumnix and
SOLA adapt scheduling or state-aware service to dynamic conditions
\cite{llumnix2024,sola2025}; Libra studies flexible request partitioning
\cite{libra2026}; Mooncake and Strata emphasize KV-centric or hierarchical
context caching~\cite{mooncake2025,strata2025}; MorphServe changes model
precision and KV capacity under pressure~\cite{morphserve2025}; and DynamoLLM
reconfigures cluster resources for performance and energy
\cite{dynamollm2025}. BurstGPT and the Azure traces provide workload
evidence~\cite{burstgpt2025,azurepublicdataset2023}, and a recent longitudinal
study by Nixon et al. examines workload evolution, caching, and load balancing
over a year of production traffic~\cite{yearllmserving2026}.

ServeGen characterizes and generates production LLM
workloads~\cite{servegen2025}, and OpenTela unifies decentralized computing
resources for heterogeneous LLM serving~\cite{opentela2026}. LMetric combines a
KV-aware indicator with an instance-load indicator, reports production-style
workloads, and derives conditions under which its multiplicative combination
can fail~\cite{lmetric2026}. These works address how to build, evaluate, or
operate serving mechanisms. The question here precedes them: in a fixed
workload and resource regime, do the default and its alternatives expose
different executable actions at all, and does forcing one alternative have
default-relative causal value?

Recent papers in \emph{Performance Evaluation} address neighboring problems:
job assignment in machine-learning inference systems with accuracy
constraints~\cite{choudhury2025job}, dispatching policies in data-center
clusters studied on Google and Alibaba workloads~\cite{yildiz2026dispatching},
resource-aware latency prediction for GPU-accelerated model
serving~\cite{liang2026lilou}, and adaptive machine-learning training on
serverless clouds~\cite{ali2025enabling}. They concern the design, evaluation,
or performance prediction of assignment, dispatching, and resource-management
mechanisms. This paper asks whether, in a given workload and resource state, an
alternative scheduling action exists and has causal one-step value relative to
a strong default, before a mechanism for choosing among actions is designed.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Evidence hierarchy and workload inventory}
We report four evidence tiers. Tier 1 is native production-derived trace replay.
Tier 2 is production-derived aggregate or window-level evaluation. Tier 3 is a
controlled transformation of a real trace, such as one-axis resource caps.
Tier 4 is synthetic or mechanistic workload construction. RQ1 uses Tier 1;
RQ2 uses Tiers 2--3; fresh causal headroom uses a preregistered Tier-3
intervention over untouched windows; the vLLM result is a bounded system
correspondence probe.

The faithful corpus contains 20 windows from each of Azure 2023 code, Azure 2023
conversation, and BurstGPT. Source order and token-length/arrival structure
are retained. Service-level objective (SLO) and class overlays are
deterministic simulator inputs;
future actual output lengths are hidden from policies. The fresh confirmatory
campaign uses 20 untouched windows per workload family for support mapping,
with causal labeling limited to the frozen selected regimes. BurstGPT did not
contribute a selected fresh causal disagreement regime.

The historical six-policy portfolio contains full prefill, small-chunk prefill,
estimated-service-first (ESTF), weighted fair sharing (WFS), least-laxity
first (LLF), and a KV-constrained policy. The six-policy portfolio is fixed for
a default-relative characterization; it is not an exhaustive set of current
schedulers.

\subsection{Canonical action opportunity}
Let $\mathcal{A}_6(s)$ be the set of canonical executable actions produced by
the six portfolio policies at state $s$, after action deduplication. A
canonical action is the simulator-executable admission, batch, and request
ordering decision after infeasible or semantically identical variants have
been collapsed. The default scheduler is the SBS, the fixed best single policy
used as the canonical baseline for the frozen replay protocol; it is not refit
per state. Let $a_{\sbs}(s)$ be its action. We define
\begin{equation}
  D(s) = \mathbf{1}\left[\exists a\in\mathcal{A}_6(s):
  a\ne a_{\sbs}(s)\right].
  \label{eq:disagreement}
\end{equation}
The opportunity prevalence is $P(D)=\sum_sD(s)/|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{all}}|$,
where the denominator is every valid SBS decision state, not only states
where policy scores differ. A policy ranking change that produces the same
admission, batch, and request order is action collapse, not opportunity.
An alternative action is any executable member of $\mathcal{A}_6(s)$ that is
not equal to $a_{\sbs}(s)$.

This definition matters operationally. Whole-policy comparisons can show a
latency or goodput difference accumulated over many decisions, but cannot say
whether an alternative existed at a particular default state. Conversely,
action diversity alone says nothing about usefulness.

\subsection{One-step causal headroom}
For every disagreement state $s$ and canonical alternative $a$, we clone the
state, force exactly one action, and return to the SBS for the continuation. Denote
the resulting continuation value by $Q_{\sbs}(s,a)$. This is a local
counterfactual, not selector performance: it estimates the one-step effect of
changing the current action while holding the subsequent default policy fixed.

For the fresh primary latency endpoint, continuation mean latency includes
active, queued, and future requests belonging to the continuation, excluding
requests completed before $s$. We define
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 A_{\mathrm{LAT}}(s,a)&=L_{\sbs}(s)-L_{\mathrm{CF}}(s,a),\\
 H_{\mathrm{LAT}}(s)&=\max\left(0,\max_aA_{\mathrm{LAT}}(s,a)\right),
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}
where positive values reduce mean latency. The beneficial-state indicator is
$B_{\mathrm{LAT}}(s)=1$ iff $\max_aA_{\mathrm{LAT}}(s,a)>0$. We report
$P(B_{\mathrm{LAT}}\mid D)$ and mean $H_{\mathrm{LAT}}$ separately from
$P(D)$. The conceptual aggregate opportunity is therefore
\begin{equation}
 P(D)\times P(B_{\mathrm{LAT}}\mid D)\times
 \text{effect magnitude},
 \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
not an exact deployable-performance equation. A high conditional benefit can
coexist with a tiny overall opportunity.

The confirmatory mean uses a faithful-window clustered percentile bootstrap
with 2,000 replicates and seed 20260920. The cluster unit is the original fresh trace
window, identified by workload source and window index, contributing
disagreement states, so multiple states from one window do not become
independent experimental units. Its verdict requires positive mean headroom
and a clustered 95\% lower endpoint above zero.

A pressure condition is a workload window plus one frozen one-axis
intervention, such as a KV-capacity cap, active-sequence cap, or arrival-rate
scaling factor. ``Support'' and ``opportunity'' refer only to the existence of
executable non-SBS actions under that condition; usefulness requires the
separate causal latency test above.

\subsection{Post hoc robustness analyses}
\label{sec:posthoc}
The analyses in Section~\ref{sec:robust} were specified after the fresh
outcomes were known. They qualify the preregistered result and do not replace
it: the endpoint $H_{\mathrm{LAT}}$, the strict criterion $B_{\mathrm{LAT}}$,
the 720 states, and the selected regimes are unchanged. A \emph{window} is a
workload source and window index (36 windows contribute states); a
\emph{regime} is a workload, pressure axis, and pressure setting pooled over
windows (five selected regimes).

\emph{Practical thresholds.} For $\epsilon\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5,1,2,5\}$~ms we
report the number and share of disagreement states with
$H_{\mathrm{LAT}}(s)>\epsilon$, that is \mbox{$P(H_{\mathrm{LAT}}>\epsilon\mid D)$},
and the number of windows that contain at least one such state.

\emph{Numerical residue.} The preregistered criterion is applied unchanged in
double precision. Simulated latency differences are integer multiples of the
decode step divided by the request population, so no genuine advantage is
smaller than about $5.2\times10^{-6}$~s. Only to identify floating-point
residue, we also count states whose best advantage exceeds $10^{-12}$~s.

\emph{Aggregation.} For a unit $u$ (a window, regime, or workload source) let
$D_u$ be its disagreement states and
$\bar H_u=|D_u|^{-1}\sum_{s\in D_u}H_{\mathrm{LAT}}(s)$. The preregistered
estimate is the \emph{state-weighted mean}
$\bar H=|D|^{-1}\sum_{s\in D}H_{\mathrm{LAT}}(s)$, in which every state has
weight $1/|D|$. The \emph{equal-window}, \emph{equal-regime}, and
\emph{equal-workload} means are $U^{-1}\sum_u\bar H_u$ over the 36 windows,
five regimes, or two workloads, so every unit has weight $1/U$ however many
states it contains. These are different estimands, not alternative estimates
of one quantity; the beneficial share is aggregated in the same way. Intervals
for the alternative means use the same window-clustered percentile bootstrap
with 200,000 replicates. For the equal-regime and equal-workload means, regimes
and workloads are fixed strata and window--regime cells are resampled within
each stratum, which ignores dependence between regimes that share a window.

\emph{Leave-one-out and concentration.} For each window (36) and each regime
(5) we recompute the state-weighted mean and beneficial share from the
remaining states, with a window-clustered bootstrap interval (200,000
replicates). Concentration is the share of the total summed headroom
$\sum_sH_{\mathrm{LAT}}(s)$ contributed by each window and regime, and the
effective number of windows $1/\sum_wp_w^2$, where $p_w$ is the share of
window $w$.

\emph{Interval diagnostics.} To check the preregistered interval we repeat the
percentile bootstrap with $10^6$ replicates, add a bias-corrected and
accelerated (BCa) interval, and compute a delete-one-window jackknife $t$
interval. These are diagnostics, not replacements.

\subsection{Objective audit}
The historical objective is arrival-normalized weighted goodput (ANWG),
\begin{equation}
 \anwg = \frac{\sum_iw_i\mathbf{1}[i\text{ completes by its SLO}]}{\sum_iw_i}.
 \label{eq:anwg}
\end{equation}
The earlier terminal arrival-normalized weighted goodput result is retained as
an objective audit, not as fresh latency evidence. The latency study uses Eq.~\ref{eq:latency}; its
mean-latency result is confirmatory only for the fresh untouched causal
universe.

\section{Results: Native Action Opportunity}
\label{sec:phasea}

Under the frozen abundant-resource baseline, all three
production-derived workloads were canonically action-null (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}
summarizes the evidence chain; Figure~\ref{fig:disagreement} gives the rates).
Azure code had 0 disagreements in
99,992 SBS decision states; Azure conversation had 0 in 461,985; and BurstGPT
had 0 in 440,461. The corresponding maximum KV utilizations were 0.0027,
0.0017, and 0.0038, with no active-cap binding states. Policy-ranking proxy
scores differed in a small number of states, but the canonical actions
collapsed to the SBS action. In this resource regime the six-policy portfolio
offered no executable alternative.

\section{Results: Pressure-Induced Disagreement}
\label{sec:phaseb}

The pressure map evaluated 1,080 one-axis conditions: 20 windows per workload
family, each under settings on the active-sequence, KV-capacity, and
arrival-rate axes (Figure~\ref{fig:transition}; Table~\ref{tab:transition}).
Of these, 926 conditions were valid and unconstrained, 38 partially
constrained, 96 strongly constrained, and 20 horizon-invalid. Across valid
conditions, 11,328 canonical disagreement states were observed.

Arrival scaling through $8\times$ remained action-null for all three workload
families. Active-sequence pressure created disagreement at a cap of 16
(BurstGPT), 8 (Azure code), or 4 (Azure conversation), and KV pressure created
it at a capacity of 16,000 tokens in all three workloads
(Table~\ref{tab:transition}). Disagreement became sustained, in at
least two windows, at caps of 8 (BurstGPT) and 4 (Azure code and conversation)
and at KV capacities of 16,000 (Azure code and BurstGPT) and 8,000 (Azure
conversation).

\section{Results: Fresh Support and Causal Latency Headroom}
\label{sec:fresh}

The fresh support mapping used untouched windows and the frozen pressure
protocol. The causal labeling plan selected five Azure code and conversation
regimes (active-sequence and KV-capacity caps) with 720 disagreement states.
BurstGPT contributed no selected causal regime.

The confirmatory intervention found 590 beneficial
states (Table~\ref{tab:fresh}; Figure~\ref{fig:fresh}), giving
\begin{equation*}
 P(B_{\mathrm{LAT}}\mid D)=590/720=0.8194\ (81.9\%).
\end{equation*}
Mean oracle latency headroom was 0.001995791 s (1.9958 ms), with a clustered
95\% confidence interval (CI) of [0.000190884, 0.003546196] s
([0.1909, 3.5462] ms). The
preregistered verdict was positive. The result is statistically supported but
operationally modest: it establishes measurable default-relative latency
opportunity at disagreement states, whereas whether its magnitude exceeds the
engineering cost of adaptation is a separate question. The conditional share is
defined on disagreement states only, and because $P(D)$ is often below one
percent in the selected regimes it is not the share of all scheduler decisions
that could be improved.

These preregistered values are state-weighted over disagreement states drawn
from 36 windows, and they should be read with the post hoc analyses of
Section~\ref{sec:robust}: the typical state has a much smaller headroom
(median 0.20~ms), and the mean is highly concentrated in one window. One of the
590 beneficial states has an advantage of $5.6\times10^{-17}$~s, exactly one
unit in the last place of the 0.357~s latencies compared, with both branches
completing the same requests; a post hoc guard of $10^{-12}$~s gives
589/720 (81.8\%) and changes the mean by less than $10^{-19}$~s.

\subsection{Opportunity decomposition}
Figure~\ref{fig:regimemap} makes the distinction between prevalence and value
concrete.
Azure-code KV capacity = 16,000 has the largest selected $P(D)$ and the largest mean
headroom; active-cap regimes have much smaller $P(D)$ and correspondingly
smaller aggregate opportunity even when some disagreement states are
beneficial. A practitioner should therefore measure all three factors in
Eq.~\ref{eq:decomposition}: whether a different action exists, whether it
helps conditional on existing, and whether its effect is large enough to
matter relative to system noise and control overhead.

The practical reading is deliberately staged. Under abundant resources, this
study observed no canonical disagreement and therefore no demonstrated action
support for adaptive selection. Under constrained KV or active-sequence
regimes, disagreement emerges and creates an executable opportunity. Within
those disagreement states, alternatives with positive headroom are frequent
but mostly small (Section~\ref{sec:robust}). Opportunity and headroom still do
not prove that a deployed adaptive selector is worthwhile.

\section{Results: Robustness and Concentration of Causal Headroom}
\label{sec:robust}

The preregistered result of Section~\ref{sec:fresh} is a state-weighted mean
over 720 disagreement states drawn from 36 windows. This section asks, post
hoc, how large the headroom is in a typical state and how far the mean depends
on which windows and regimes contribute (Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}). The
preregistered criterion and estimate are not modified.

\subsection{Effect scale and distribution}
Table~\ref{tab:thresholds} counts the states whose headroom exceeds each
practical threshold. The 81.9\% beneficial share is not the share of states
that offer material gains: 60.0\% of states exceed 0.1~ms, 29.4\% exceed 0.5~ms, 22.5\%
exceed 2~ms, and 17.9\% exceed 5~ms. The larger effects are also concentrated.
Above 0.5~ms, 201 of the 212 states belong to the Azure-code KV-capacity = 16,000
regime, and only 4 of the 36 windows contain a state above 2~ms.

\subsection{Sensitivity to aggregation and to individual windows and regimes}
Every aggregation of Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} yields a positive mean whose
interval excludes zero, but the magnitude depends on what is weighted: from
1.996~ms (state-weighted) to 0.327~ms (equal-window), a factor of about six. The
beneficial share is likewise composition-sensitive, from 0.819 (state-weighted)
to 0.632 (equal-window). The state-weighted mean is the preregistered
estimand and answers ``what is the expected headroom at a disagreement state
drawn from this sample''; the equal-window and equal-regime means answer the
same question for a typical window or regime, and they are not
interchangeable with it.

The sign therefore survives all 41 omissions, whereas the headline magnitude
does not survive the omission of the two dominant units.

\subsection{Concentration of headroom}
Window w11 of the Azure-code trace contains 263 of the 720 states (36.5\%) but
contributes 88.4\% of the total summed headroom; the three largest windows
contribute 95.3\%, and the effective number of windows by headroom mass is
1.27 (5.7 by state count). At regime level, Azure-code KV-capacity = 16,000
holds 59.9\% of the states and 96.8\% of the headroom, contributing 1.93 of
the 2.00~ms state-weighted mean. Within w11, 205 states of that regime average
6.16~ms. Of the 129 states above 5~ms, 128 are in w11, as are 140 of the 162
states above 2~ms (Figure~\ref{fig:robust}b). The state-weighted mean is thus
highly concentrated in a narrow high-headroom subset of states and should not
be read as the typical benefit of a disagreement state.

\subsection{Uncertainty diagnostics}
Monte Carlo error does not explain the width of the preregistered interval.
With $10^6$ replicates the percentile interval is [0.184, 3.543]~ms, no
replicate has a nonpositive mean, and the lower endpoint of the 2,000-replicate
procedure varies by only 0.004~ms (standard deviation) across 1000 seeds. The BCa
interval is [0.239, 4.049]~ms. The bootstrap distribution is bimodal: 36\% of
replicates do not draw window w11 and have a mean of about 0.36~ms, against
2.43~ms for those that do, so the percentile interval spans the gap between
two populations of replicates. The delete-one-window jackknife $t$ interval,
[$-1.36$, 5.35]~ms, includes zero because one influential window inflates its
standard error (1.65~ms); all 36 delete-one estimates are positive, and we
treat the jackknife interval as a diagnostic of this dependence rather than as
a competing interval. Uncertainty about the magnitude of the headroom thus
reflects its concentration in few windows, not simulation noise.

\section{Objective Sensitivity: Arrival-Normalized Weighted Goodput Saturation}
\label{sec:anwg}

The earlier pressure study completed with causal integrity and
complete branch coverage, but all terminal ANWG values were 1.0 and all ANWG
advantages were zero. An identifiability audit found no implementation bug:
requests completed within deadlines and the objective saturated. Simple
sensitivity cases confirmed that ANWG distinguishes missed deadlines or
unfinished work, but not different latency or queueing when all requests
still meet a loose deadline.

Most counterfactual branches nevertheless changed latency. Thus zero ANWG
headroom does not imply zero latency headroom. We do not use the earlier post hoc latency analysis as confirmatory
evidence. The fresh untouched study
in Section~\ref{sec:fresh} is the confirmatory latency result, with its own
pre-frozen primary endpoint and clustered bootstrap.

This is objective sensitivity, not evidence that ANWG is invalid. It is an appropriate deadline-goodput objective for one question; mean latency is a
different operational objective. A serving paper should not promote whichever
metric yields the more favorable conclusion.

\section{Results: Bounded vLLM System Correspondence Probe}
\label{sec:real}

The probe used one RTX 5060 Ti GPU, vLLM 0.27.1, Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, and
deterministic token-shape replay. It comprised 40 full-versus-chunked runs and
a 20-run native chunked-prefill budget probe, with queueing and scheduler
traces retained. It separates three levels of evidence. Level 1, one-step
headroom at disagreement states, is established only in the controlled replay.
Level 2, that resource pressure changes observable queueing and scheduling
behavior, is supported in vLLM. Level 3, that a deployed adaptive selector
would recover the simulated headroom or improve end-to-end latency, SLOs, or
throughput, is not established.

The native budget probe held chunked prefill fixed and varied the per-step
token budget (512 tokens, T512, versus 4,096 tokens, T4096). T512 produced
1,680 scheduler steps, 165 mixed prefill/decode steps, and 194 partial-prefill
items; T4096 produced 1,536, 55, and 9. Waiting queues reached lengths of 7 and
5, respectively, while running sequences reached 4 in both cases. The low-load
regime showed a 30.6~ms difference in late time to first token and a 16.3~ms
prompt-heavy end-to-end tradeoff; the high-load regime showed a smaller
time-to-first-token difference and a 23.3~ms prompt-heavy end-to-end tradeoff.

The direct full-versus-chunked comparison did not reproduce the simulator's
qualitative reversal (Table~\ref{tab:real}). KV utilization and preemption
metrics were unavailable in the native probe because its metric parser returned
empty dictionaries. The probe therefore shows that concrete serving pressure
alters observable scheduling behavior and latency; it does not estimate
Eq.~\ref{eq:latency} in the real system.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Sign and magnitude of the headroom}
The fresh study establishes that positive one-step headroom exists: under the
preregistered criterion, forcing an alternative action lowers mean latency in
590 of 720 disagreement states (81.9\%). The sign is stable across the
aggregations and leave-one-out analyses of Section~\ref{sec:robust}. The
magnitude is not. The preregistered state-weighted mean of 2.00~ms (95\%
window-clustered CI [0.19, 3.55]~ms) estimates the expected headroom at a
state drawn from this sample, not the headroom of a typical state: the median
is 0.20~ms, and the mean falls to 1.36, 0.76, and 0.33~ms when workloads,
regimes, or windows receive equal weight (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}).
``Beneficial'' is a criterion on the sign of the causal effect, not on its
operational size: 29.4\% of disagreement states offer more than 0.5~ms and
22.5\% more than 2~ms (Table~\ref{tab:thresholds}).

The mean reflects where the headroom lies. One Azure-code window carries 88.4\%
of the total headroom, the Azure-code KV-capacity = 16,000 regime carries
96.8\%, and the effective number of windows by headroom mass is 1.27
(Figure~\ref{fig:robust}). Without that window the mean is 0.36~ms (95\% CI
[0.16, 0.68]~ms); without that regime it is 0.16~ms (95\% CI [0.06,
0.23]~ms). The window is part of the valid fresh sample. It shows that an
aggregate mean can conceal highly localized scheduling opportunity, so the
distribution of effects, the prevalence of material effects, and the
sensitivity to aggregation belong next to any overall mean.

The interval diagnostics agree. A $10^6$-replicate percentile interval
([0.18, 3.54]~ms) matches the preregistered one, and the BCa interval
([0.24, 4.05]~ms) stays positive, so Monte Carlo error does not limit the
inference. The delete-one-window jackknife interval includes zero, and the
bootstrap distribution is bimodal according to whether window w11 is drawn.
Both reflect dependence on one cluster: with 36 windows but only about one
effective window carrying the headroom, the preregistered interval is wide
because cluster diversity is limited. It remains the pre-specified inference;
the diagnostics locate the source of its width.

\subsection{Opportunity, prediction, and deployment}
Adaptive scheduling should be evaluated in stages, because each stage can fail
while the previous one holds. Resource pressure need not produce executable
disagreement: native replay met no alternative action in about one million
decision states. Disagreement need not be beneficial, a positive effect need
not be material, and a material oracle effect need not be recoverable.
Collapsing the stages overstates opportunity at every step: a virtual-best-solver
gap or complementarity in policy scores does not show that the live system
reaches states with a different executable action, and an oracle mean of about
2~ms reads as the value of adaptation although most disagreement states offer a
fraction of it.

The estimand is one-step oracle headroom under SBS continuation: one
alternative action is forced at a disagreement state, and the default then
resumes. $H_{\mathrm{LAT}}(s)$ selects the best alternative after the outcomes
are observed, so it answers whether a useful action existed at $s$, not whether
an online system could have identified it, and it does not measure the value of
a fully adaptive policy, of longer-horizon switching, of a selector, or of
adaptation overhead. Those are the later stages of Eq.~\ref{eq:chain};
separating opportunity from predictability lets a weak or absent opportunity
rule out selector effort before it is spent.

\subsection{Resource pressure and action support}
Arrival-rate scaling up to eight times left every workload family action-null,
whereas active-sequence and KV-capacity caps created disagreement in all three
(Section~\ref{sec:phaseb}). In these production-derived workloads under
controlled resource interventions, opportunity is tied to particular forms of
resource binding rather than to load as a scalar. Workload intensity and
resource contention are different quantities: a system can be busy without
facing a scheduling choice, and can face one at the native arrival rate once a
capacity constraint binds. The relevant operating region is defined by which
resource binds, not by arrival rate alone.

\subsection{System correspondence}
The vLLM experiment is a bounded system correspondence probe
(Section~\ref{sec:real}). It shows that resource pressure changes observable
queueing and scheduling behavior in a real engine: a smaller chunked-prefill
token budget produced more mixed prefill--decode steps and more partial-prefill
items than a larger one. It did not reproduce the simulator's qualitative
reversal in the direct full-versus-chunked comparison, it has no exact one-step
intervention, and it does not validate the simulated headroom magnitude or an
adaptive-scheduler gain. The probe supports the qualitative link between
resource pressure and scheduling behavior; the magnitude question is answered
only by the simulated counterfactuals.

\subsection{Implications for practice}
For a system designer, the evidence supports five practices.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Measure executable action disagreement before building a selector;
  diversity in policy scores is not evidence of it.
  \item Characterize which resource-binding regimes the system enters, rather
  than only its arrival rate.
  \item Report the distribution of effect sizes, the prevalence of the states
  where they occur, and their concentration, alongside the oracle mean.
  \item Weigh the recoverable gain against selector, monitoring, and
  switching costs; the median disagreement state offers only 0.20~ms.
  \item Outside the few regimes with material opportunity, staying with a fixed
  scheduler may be the appropriate policy.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Relation to modern adaptive systems}
LMetric, SOLA, Llumnix, MorphServe, Strata, Libra, and FastServe address how
to adapt serving behavior under dynamic resource and SLO conditions; ServeGen,
BurstGPT, the Azure traces, OpenTela, and longitudinal trace studies address
how to obtain and operate on realistic workload evidence. The staged
measurement here is complementary. It identifies the operating regions in which
such mechanisms have an executable alternative to exploit, and it quantifies
the headroom that a strong default leaves there.

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}
The limitations below bound what the evidence supports.
\begin{description}
  \item[Policy portfolio.] The six policies are not the universe of
  schedulers, and other portfolios may expose different action support. The
  results are default-relative measurements for this portfolio.
  \item[Workloads.] Three production-derived families were studied. Native
  replay and pressure mapping cover all three, but fresh causal labeling covers
  selected Azure regimes because BurstGPT contributed no selected regime.
  \item[Controlled interventions.] Active-sequence and KV caps are
  experimental interventions, not observed production provisioning. They
  identify which forms of binding create disagreement, not how often production
  systems operate under them.
  \item[One-step oracle.] $H_{\mathrm{LAT}}$ isolates one-step causal
  opportunity with an ex post best alternative; it is not closed-loop
  adaptation.
  \item[Concentration.] The magnitude rests on few windows, with an effective
  number of about 1.27 by headroom mass. The sign has broader support: 32 of the
  36 windows contain a state with positive headroom.
  \item[System correspondence.] The probe uses one model, one GPU, one vLLM
  version, token-shape replay, and incomplete instrumentation (KV utilization
  and preemption metrics were unavailable), so it supports only the qualitative
  mechanism.
  \item[Objective.] Mean latency is the primary causal endpoint. The goodput
  audit shows that the objective changes the measured opportunity
  (Section~\ref{sec:anwg}); tail-latency and SLO-violation objectives were not
  evaluated.
  \item[External validity.] Other models, hardware, schedulers, context-length
  distributions, batching systems, and resource managers may show a different
  opportunity structure. The staged protocol transfers; the measured
  magnitudes need not.
\end{description}
Directions that follow from the decomposition are to learn a conservative
selector only in regimes where measured opportunity is material, to test
whether those regimes recur on other hardware and models, and to extend the
counterfactual to longer horizons and tail-latency objectives with explicit
accounting of adaptation overhead.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper asked when adaptive scheduling has anything to act on in LLM serving, before any adaptive scheduler is built. Replay of production-derived traces under abundant resources offered essentially nothing: none of about one million decision states had an executable alternative to the single best scheduler (SBS). Controlled active-sequence and KV-capacity caps, but not arrival-rate scaling up to eight times, created such states, and fresh one-step interventions showed that forcing an alternative there lowers latency in most of them (590 of 720 under the preregistered criterion). A bounded vLLM probe supports the qualitative link between pressure and scheduling behavior without validating the simulated magnitudes.

The headroom is positive but small and concentrated. The state-weighted mean of 2.00~ms is not a typical effect: the median disagreement state offers 0.20~ms, the mean falls to 0.33~ms when windows are weighted equally, and one trace window holds 88.4\% of the total. Adaptation opportunity is therefore real, sparse, pressure-dependent, and generally modest, and its existence does not imply deployment value.

The broader lesson is that scheduler adaptation should be justified stage by stage, from resource pressure through executable disagreement and causal benefit to the distribution and prevalence of the effect, before selector complexity is added. Reporting concentration and aggregation sensitivity, and not only a mean, distinguishes a localized opportunity from a general one. Adaptation is justified by where the measured opportunity lies, not by its average.
